Overview

An ancient framework, applied clinically today

Early Muslim scholars treated the human psyche (tibb al-rūḥānī) as a subdiscipline of medicine, with one of the world's earliest documented hospitals founded in 9th-century Baghdad. TIIP, developed by Hooman Keshavarzi and Fahad Khan and practiced at the Khalil Center, revives this tradition — formalizing it into a clinical model for the modern therapy room.

Health is a continuum, not a diagnosis

TIIP rejects the idea that wellness is simply the absence of disorder. A person with no clinical diagnosis can still be spiritually unwell — afflicted by illnesses the secular model has no name for, like hasad (corrosive envy) or shuḥḥ (avarice). Treatment addresses both psychological symptoms and these spiritual afflictions, together.

Foundations

Three sources of knowledge, one science of the soul

Unlike frameworks that draw a hard line between "sacred" and "secular" knowledge, TIIP treats empirical, rational, and revealed knowledge as equally legitimate — graded by the strength of their evidence rather than their origin.

🔬

Empirical

al-ʿilm al-ḥissī

Knowledge acquired through observation, sensory experience, and clinical study.

🧭

Rational

al-ʿilm al-ʿaqlī

Truths reached through human reason — whether self-evident (badīhī) or inferred (istidlālī).

📖

Revealed

al-naql

Verified truth from scripture (Qur'an) and prophetic tradition (Sunnah).

Every claim — from any source — is graded by certainty:

Definitive qaṭʿī
Probabilistic ẓannī

This grading lets TIIP place any piece of knowledge — Western or Eastern, ancient or new — appropriately within a single coherent model of the psyche.

Underlying assumptions of the model

  • Fiṭrah — every human is born with an innate inclination toward good and connection with the divine.
  • Multidimensional — the psyche has several interwoven layers; no single approach fits everyone.
  • Interconnected — change one element of the psyche, and the others shift in response.
  • Afterlife-aware — assessment and treatment consider humanity's primordial origin and final return.
  • Clinician matters — the practitioner's own inner state shapes the process of change as much as the patient's.

The Therapeutic Relationship

Not a hierarchy. A companionship.

رفيق Rafīq

TIIP describes the practitioner not as a detached expert but as a Rafīq — an "intimate friend." The word comes from rifq, meaning gentle companionship: a relationship that is collaborative and authoritative, never hierarchical or cold.

Crucially, the Rafīq is understood to be on their own parallel journey of growth. A practitioner is expected to stay aware of their own inner deficiencies and biases, working on their own wellbeing so it never bleeds into the care they provide.

Case Illustration

Harris: the model in practice

Harris, a 30-year-old man, sought therapy for marital conflict and anger outbursts rooted in anxiety and depression. His case shows how every layer of the psyche shows up — and how each was addressed in turn. Click each element to see how it appeared in his case.

Harris's primary issue began with his iḥsās — emotions of sadness from unmet needs for connection, which surfaced as anxiety and explosive anger whenever he felt overwhelmed or out of control.

Outcome

Through emotional awareness work, behavioral pattern recognition, cognitive reframing rooted in his own religious education, and spiritual practices (prayer, acceptance of God's decree), Harris's symptom scores improved. He gained better control over his anger and learned to hold boundaries with his wife without swinging to emotional extremes.

Become a Practitioner

The TIIP Certification Process

Offered through the Khalil Center's School of Islamic Psychology, certification moves a clinician from foundational study through advanced application to supervised, evaluated practice.

Level I

Foundational Study

Introduction to the foundations of the TIIP modality along with a deep dive into the core principles of the model. This includes holistic schemas of health and pathology, nature of the human being, composition of the human psyche, principles of change, and role of the TIIP practitioner.

Level II

Advanced Application

Advanced application of the TIIP modality across varying treatment settings and problems.

Level III

Supervised Practice

200 supervised practice hours under the supervision of a TIIP supervisor through the treatment of at least 10 therapy cases where TIIP was utilized.

Case Conceptualization

Upon completion of all three levels, candidates present a case study to a panel of three TIIP-certified practitioners (including their supervisor), who evaluate their adherence to and capacity to utilize the framework.

Requirements

  • 1Completion of all 3 levels and a successful TIIP case presentation.
  • 2Licensure or license eligibility in mental health practice, or Islamic clergy with formal pastoral training.
  • 3Evidence of completion of the minimum religious education obligatory upon every Muslim (farḍ al-ʿayn) — a letter from a religious teacher or a certificate from an Islamic seminary suffices.

Source: Khalil Center, School of Islamic Psychology — TIIP Certification.

Conclusion

Healing the whole person — mind, body, and soul

TIIP doesn't replace psychotherapy with religion, nor religion with psychotherapy. It holds both, on the conviction that a reductionistic approach to the human being — addressing only thought, or only behavior, or only chemistry — will always overlook something. The goal is qalb salīm: a heart at peace, integrated, and whole.
